# The Life of Projects: How Work Is Conceived, Realized, and Maintained — An Interdisciplinary Integrative Review

## TL;DR
- Across ten disciplines the same finding recurs: the object of a project is not fixed at conception but is continuously re-specified through action, so the gap between roadmap and reality is not a failure of planning but the normal ontology of projects — best understood through complementary lenses of emergent strategy (Mintzberg & Waters 1985), effectuation (Sarasvathy 2001), routine dynamics (Feldman & Pentland 2003), organizational becoming (Tsoukas & Chia 2002), and "programming as theory building" (Naur 1985).
- Individuals and groups co-produce this evolution through boundary objects, translation, communities of practice, genres, and sensemaking; coordination artifacts (plans, roadmaps, records) function less as control instruments than as shared reference points that are themselves reworked as understanding deepens.
- The literatures are rigorous but structurally biased toward large Western organizations and formal project settings; solo practitioners and very small structures are almost absent, meaning the individual managing concurrent IT projects must extrapolate from theory built on other units of analysis — a caveat this review makes explicit rather than papering over.

## Review Method
This is an **integrative review** in the sense of Torraco (2005), whose aim is not exhaustive coverage of a mature and settled field but the critical synthesis and reconceptualization of a fragmented, cross-disciplinary body of work into an integrated framework. Because the topic — how projects are conceived, realized, and maintained — is dispersed across strategy, organization studies, science and technology studies (STS), design theory, software engineering, information and archival science, psychology, and linguistics, a systematic (PRISMA-style) protocol would have been inappropriate: no single database or keyword set spans these vocabularies, and the constructs (drift, emergence, sensemaking, translation) are named differently in each. The corpus was therefore constructed through **purposive seeding plus bibliographic chaining**: a set of foundational, field-defining works was identified a priori, each citation was verified against primary sources (author, title, year, venue, pages, DOI), and the corpus was then extended both **backward** (mining the bibliographies of the seed works) and **forward** (examining recent literature citing the seed works, e.g. the routine-dynamics stream after Feldman & Pentland 2003, the revival of Naur 1985 in the software-craft and LLM-era discourse, and the continuing Rethinking Project Management program). The epistemological stance combines methodological rigor with structural critique: the review reports what is well-evidenced, flags what is speculative or contested, and interrogates whose work and which organizational forms these literatures systematically over- or under-represent.

## Key Findings

1. **The object evolves; it does not merely get built.** Every strand converges on a processual ontology: the artifact-in-becoming is re-specified as it is realized. Strategy "emerges" (Mintzberg & Waters), programs are "theories" that live in people's heads and die when they leave (Naur), software must change or become progressively less useful (Lehman), and organization itself is a continuous "becoming" (Tsoukas & Chia).
2. **Plans are resources for action, not determinants of it.** Suchman's central thesis — that plans are weak resources for situated action rather than scripts that action executes — is echoed in effectuation (means-driven, not goal-driven), in routine dynamics (the ostensive is not the performance), and in genre theory (templates are enacted, adapted, and drift).
3. **Interaction is mediated by objects.** Boundary objects, epistemic objects, records, and genres let heterogeneous actors coordinate without consensus; the object carries the collaboration and is reshaped by it.
4. **Dynamics are dialectical, not oppositional.** Structure/improvisation, planning/emergence, and stability/change are recursive couplings, not either/or choices — the clearest formal statement being Feldman & Pentland's ostensive/performative recursion.
5. **Institutionalized responses to emergence exist.** Agile/iterative development, technical-debt accounting, and the records continuum are all attempts to metabolize inevitable change rather than suppress it.
6. **A blind spot is structural.** The evidence base is dominated by large, Western, well-funded, multi-actor organizations; the solo worker and the micro-structure are theorized mostly by extrapolation.

## Details

### 1. Plans versus situated action
The foundational displacement of "plan-as-control" comes from Lucy Suchman's *Plans and Situated Actions: The Problem of Human–Machine Communication* (Cambridge University Press, 1987; reissued and expanded as *Human-Machine Reconfigurations: Plans and Situated Actions*, 2nd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2007). Drawing on ethnomethodology and a study of users struggling with a "help" system on a photocopier, Suchman argued that plans are not executed but are *resources* that actors interpret and improvise around in the concrete, contingent situation of action. The plan is retrospectively and prospectively invoked, but the action is organized in situ.

In strategy, Henry Mintzberg and James A. Waters, "Of Strategies, Deliberate and Emergent," *Strategic Management Journal*, 6(3), 257–272 (1985, https://doi.org/10.1002/smj.4250060306), proposed deliberate and emergent strategy as two ends of a continuum, distinguishing intended, realized, and unrealized strategies and cataloguing eight types (planned, entrepreneurial, ideological, umbrella, process, unconnected, consensus, imposed). Their key move — that realized strategy is a blend of intention and emergence, and that "umbrella" and "process" strategies deliberately leave content to emerge — directly models the roadmap/reality gap.

Saras D. Sarasvathy, "Causation and Effectuation: Toward a Theoretical Shift from Economic Inevitability to Entrepreneurial Contingency," *Academy of Management Review*, 26(2), 243–263 (2001, https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.2001.4378020), inverts means-ends logic: causal reasoning starts from a goal and seeks means; effectual reasoning starts from available means (who I am, what I know, whom I know) and allows goals to emerge, governed by affordable loss rather than expected return, by partnerships rather than competitive analysis, and by leveraging contingency ("lemonade") rather than avoiding it. Effectuation is the decision logic of conception under genuine (Knightian) uncertainty.

### 2. Process organization studies
Haridimos Tsoukas and Robert Chia, "On Organizational Becoming: Rethinking Organizational Change," *Organization Science*, 13(5), 567–582 (2002, https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.13.5.567.7810), treat change as the normal condition of organizational life — "the reweaving of actors' webs of beliefs and habits of action to accommodate new experiences" — and stability as the achievement that must be explained. Karl E. Weick's *Sensemaking in Organizations* (Sage, Foundations for Organizational Science series, 1995) supplies the micro-mechanism: people enact the environments they then interpret, retrospectively imposing order on equivocal experience; sensemaking is grounded in identity, social, ongoing, retrospective, cue-driven, and driven by plausibility rather than accuracy. Ann Langley, "Strategies for Theorizing from Process Data," *Academy of Management Review*, 24(4), 691–710 (1999, https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1999.2553248), provides the methodological backbone (seven sensemaking strategies — narrative, quantification, alternate templates, grounded theory, visual mapping, temporal bracketing, synthetic), and Tor Hernes's process-philosophical work extends the ontology.

### 3. Routine dynamics
Martha S. Feldman and Brian T. Pentland, "Reconceptualizing Organizational Routines as a Source of Flexibility and Change," *Administrative Science Quarterly*, 48(1), 94–118 (2003, https://doi.org/10.2307/3556620), is the pivot. Adapting Latour's ostensive/performative distinction, they argue routines have an *ostensive* aspect (the abstract, generalized idea of the routine — "what we typically think of as the structure") and a *performative* aspect (specific actions by specific people at specific times and places). The two are recursively coupled: the ostensive guides and accounts for performances, while performances create, maintain, and modify the ostensive. This dissolves the old opposition between routine-as-inertia and change, and it is the sharpest available model for how a roadmap (ostensive) and daily execution (performative) continually rewrite each other. The subsequent routine-dynamics program (see the *Organization Science* special issue introduction, "Beyond Routines as Things," 2016, and the *Cambridge Handbook of Routine Dynamics*, eds. Feldman, Pentland, D'Adderio, Dittrich, Rerup & Seidl, Cambridge University Press, 2021) treats routines as generative, situated, patterned accomplishments.

### 4. Critical project management research
The "Rethinking Project Management" agenda emerged from a UK EPSRC-funded network (2004–2006) led by Mark Winter. Two capstone papers appeared in a special issue of the *International Journal of Project Management*, both in vol. 24, no. 8 (2006): Mark Winter, Charles Smith, Peter Morris and Svetlana Cicmil, "Directions for future research in project management: The main findings of a UK government-funded research network," pp. 638–649 (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijproman.2006.08.009); and Winter, Smith, Cooke-Davies and Cicmil, "The importance of 'process' in Rethinking Project Management: The story of a UK Government-funded research network," pp. 650–662. The companion actuality paper is Svetlana Cicmil, Terry Williams, Janice Thomas and Damian Hodgson, "Rethinking Project Management: Researching the actuality of projects," *International Journal of Project Management*, 24(8), 675–686 (2006), which calls for studying the lived experience of projects rather than idealized models. (Note the verified authorship nuance: the "Directions" paper is Winter, Smith, Morris & Cicmil; the "process/story" companion substitutes Cooke-Davies for Morris.) The foundational temporary-organizing reference is Rolf A. Lundin and Anders Söderholm, "A Theory of the Temporary Organization," *Scandinavian Journal of Management*, 11(4), 437–455 (1995, https://doi.org/10.1016/0956-5221(95)00036-U), which builds a theory around action (not decision) and four concepts — time, task, team, and transition — and the notion of "institutionalized termination." The "projects-as-practice" stream extends strategy-as-practice into this domain. Related: Damian Hodgson and Svetlana Cicmil (eds.), *Making Projects Critical* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2006).

### 5. Design theory
Horst W. J. Rittel and Melvin M. Webber, "Dilemmas in a General Theory of Planning," *Policy Sciences*, 4(2), 155–169 (1973, https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01405730), introduced "wicked problems" — problems with no definitive formulation, no stopping rule, solutions that are better/worse not true/false, no immediate or ultimate test, every attempt consequential ("no trial and error"), and no enumerable solution set. Donald A. Schön's *The Reflective Practitioner: How Professionals Think in Action* (Basic Books, 1983) develops "reflection-in-action" and "knowing-in-action" — the designer conducts a "reflective conversation with the situation," and the materials "talk back." Herbert A. Simon's *The Sciences of the Artificial* (MIT Press; 1st ed. 1969, 3rd ed. 1996) frames design as the science of "devising courses of action aimed at changing existing situations into preferred ones" and introduces bounded rationality and satisficing. C-K theory (Concept–Knowledge theory) was introduced by Armand Hatchuel and Benoit Weil in 2003 (Hatchuel & Weil, "A new approach of innovative design: an introduction to C-K theory," *Proceedings of ICED'03*, Stockholm, 2003) and advanced in Hatchuel & Weil, "C-K design theory: an advanced formulation," *Research in Engineering Design*, 19(4), 181–192 (2009, https://doi.org/10.1007/s00163-008-0043-4): design is the co-expansion of a Concept space and a Knowledge space, formally capturing how a project generates *both* new objects and new knowledge as it proceeds.

### 6. STS and anthropology
Susan Leigh Star and James R. Griesemer, "Institutional Ecology, 'Translations' and Boundary Objects: Amateurs and Professionals in Berkeley's Museum of Vertebrate Zoology, 1907–39," *Social Studies of Science*, 19(3), 387–420 (1989, https://doi.org/10.1177/030631289019003001), coined "boundary objects" — objects plastic enough to adapt to local needs yet robust enough to maintain a common identity across sites (they distinguish repositories, ideal types, coincident boundaries, standardized forms). Michel Callon, "Some elements of a sociology of translation: domestication of the scallops and the fishermen of St Brieuc Bay," in John Law (ed.), *Power, Action and Belief: A New Sociology of Knowledge?* (Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1986), pp. 196–233, and Bruno Latour's actor-network theory model project realization as "translation" — problematization, interessement, enrolment, mobilization — in which human and non-human actants are aligned into a network that can always betray or drift. Epistemic/"objects of knowledge" work (Karin Knorr Cetina; Hans-Jörg Rheinberger's "epistemic things") theorizes objects that are constitutively incomplete and generate further inquiry. Jean Lave and Etienne Wenger, *Situated Learning: Legitimate Peripheral Participation* (Cambridge University Press, 1991), and Wenger, *Communities of Practice: Learning, Meaning, and Identity* (Cambridge University Press, 1998), locate learning and competence in participation in practice. Edwin Hutchins, *Cognition in the Wild* (MIT Press, 1995), shows cognition distributed across people, artifacts, and representations (the ship-navigation case), dissolving the boundary between individual mind and organized activity.

### 7. Software engineering and computer science
Peter Naur, "Programming as Theory Building," *Microprocessing and Microprogramming*, 15(5), 253–261 (1985, https://doi.org/10.1016/0165-6074(85)90032-8), argues that a program is not its source code but a *theory* held by its builders about how the problem maps onto the execution; documentation and code are lossy representations. In Naur's own words, the "death of a program" occurs "when the programmer team possessing its theory is dissolved," and "revival of a program, that is reestablishing the theory of a program merely from the documentation, is strictly impossible." This thesis has been vigorously revived in the software-craft and LLM-era discourse — for example the *Future of Coding* podcast episode on the paper (Ivan Reese and Jimmy Miller), and essays such as cekrem.github.io's "Programming as Theory Building: Why Senior Developers Are More Valuable Than Ever" (which argues that "as we watch junior developers reflexively accept LLM-generated code they don't understand… Naur's central thesis becomes crystal clear: a program is not its source code") and Gareth Bowen's "AI and the Theory Building View of Programming" (which reasons that "if the team is replaced either by human or artificial intelligence the theory is lost and a complete rewrite will be the cheapest option"). Meir M. (Manny) Lehman, "Programs, Life Cycles, and Laws of Software Evolution," *Proceedings of the IEEE*, 68(9), 1060–1076 (1980), and later revisions (Lehman, 1996) distinguish S-, P- and E-type programs and state laws of software evolution — continuing change, increasing complexity, self-regulation, conservation of organizational stability, conservation of familiarity, continuing growth, declining quality, and feedback system. Melvin E. Conway, "How Do Committees Invent?", *Datamation*, 14(4), 28–31 (April 1968), gives Conway's Law: organizations are constrained to produce designs that copy their own communication structures — linking group interaction directly to artifact structure. Ward Cunningham originated the "technical debt" metaphor in his OOPSLA'92 experience report, "The WyCash Portfolio Management System," *Addendum to the Proceedings of OOPSLA '92* (ACM SIGPLAN OOPS Messenger, 4(2), 29–30, 1992, https://doi.org/10.1145/157709.157715): shipping "not-quite-right" code is like going into debt, tolerable if repaid promptly by rewriting, ruinous if the interest compounds. Agile/iterative development (Manifesto for Agile Software Development, 2001; Scrum; XP) is the institutionalized response to emergence — short cycles, working software over comprehensive documentation, responding to change over following a plan. Terry Winograd and Fernando Flores, *Understanding Computers and Cognition: A New Foundation for Design* (Ablex, 1986; later reissued by Addison-Wesley), bring speech-act theory (via Searle and Heideggerian/Maturana grounding) to coordination: organizations are networks of recurrent conversations for action (requests, promises, assertions, declarations), the basis of the "language/action perspective."

### 8. Information science and archival science
JoAnne Yates and Wanda J. Orlikowski, "Genres of Organizational Communication: A Structurational Approach to Studying Communication and Media," *Academy of Management Review*, 17(2), 299–326 (1992, https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1992.4279545), theorize genres (the memo, the report, the meeting, the roadmap, the stand-up) as typified communicative actions enacted in recurrent situations; genres are produced and reproduced through use and *evolve* — providing a fine-grained account of how coordination artifacts themselves drift. Frank Upward's records continuum model — "Structuring the Records Continuum, Part One: Postcustodial principles and properties," *Archives & Manuscripts*, 24(2), 268–285 (1996), and "Part Two: Structuration theory and recordkeeping," *Archives & Manuscripts*, 25(1), 10–35 (1997) — reconceives records not through a linear lifecycle but as a space-time continuum (create, capture, organize, pluralize), integrating structuration theory: the object of maintenance is continuously constituted, not filed and forgotten. Ikujiro Nonaka, "A Dynamic Theory of Organizational Knowledge Creation," *Organization Science*, 5(1), 14–37 (1994), and Nonaka & Takeuchi (1995), advance the SECI model (socialization, externalization, combination, internalization) of tacit/explicit knowledge conversion. Its principal critique is Stephen Gourlay, "Conceptualizing Knowledge Creation: A Critique of Nonaka's Theory," *Journal of Management Studies*, 43(7), 1415–1436 (2006, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-6486.2006.00637.x): three of the four conversion modes are not supported by evidence that cannot be explained more simply, the framework omits inherently tacit knowledge, and it rests on a radically subjective, manager-centric definition of knowledge.

### 9. Psychology
The planning fallacy originates with Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, "Intuitive prediction: Biases and corrective procedures," in S. Makridakis & S. C. Wheelwright (eds.), *TIMS Studies in Management Science*, vol. 12, pp. 313–327 (North-Holland, 1979): people systematically underestimate task completion times because they take an "inside view" (case-specific scenario building) rather than an "outside view" (base rates from comparable past projects); the corrective is reference-class forecasting. Goal-setting theory is canonically stated in Edwin A. Locke and Gary P. Latham, *A Theory of Goal Setting and Task Performance* (Prentice Hall, 1990), and Locke & Latham, "Building a Practically Useful Theory of Goal Setting and Task Motivation: A 35-Year Odyssey," *American Psychologist*, 57(9), 705–717 (2002, https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705): specific, challenging goals outperform vague "do your best" goals. Its influential critique is Lisa D. Ordóñez, Maurice E. Schweitzer, Adam D. Galinsky and Max H. Bazerman, "Goals Gone Wild: The Systematic Side Effects of Over-Prescribing Goal Setting," *Academy of Management Perspectives*, 23(1), 6–16 (2009, https://doi.org/10.5465/amp.2009.37007999; also Harvard Business School NOM working paper 09-083): overly specific/challenging goals narrow focus, neglect non-goal areas, encourage unethical behavior, distort risk, corrode culture, and reduce intrinsic motivation. The tension between rigid goal-setting and emergent, effectual, situated action is one of the review's central dynamics.

### 10. Linguistics
Speech-act theory (J. L. Austin's *How to Do Things with Words*, 1962; John Searle's *Speech Acts*, 1969) underlies the Winograd–Flores language/action perspective and the "conversation for action" model of coordination. Narrative approaches — Barbara Czarniawska, *Narrating the Organization: Dramas of Institutional Identity* (University of Chicago Press, 1997), and *A Narrative Approach to Organization Studies* (Sage, 1998) — treat organizing as continuous storytelling through which actors emplot equivocal events, construct identity, and make projects intelligible over time; narrative is the form in which sensemaking is publicly accomplished and in which drift and bifurcation are retrospectively rationalized.

### Synthesis: three questions, one processual answer
**(1) How does the object evolve?** It co-evolves with the knowledge of those making it. C-K theory formalizes this (concept and knowledge spaces expand together); Naur says the "theory of the program" grows in builders' minds while code lags; Lehman says E-type systems must change or decay; Rittel & Webber say wicked problems have no fixed formulation. The roadmap is a snapshot of an ostensive that the performative will overwrite (Feldman & Pentland). Conception, realization, and maintenance are not sequential phases but overlapping registers of one continuous re-specification — precisely the records-continuum insight against the lifecycle model.

**(2) How do individuals and groups interact?** Through objects and genres. Boundary objects (Star & Griesemer) let differently-motivated actors cooperate without agreeing; translation (Callon/Latour) aligns human and non-human actants into fragile networks; communities of practice (Lave & Wenger) locate competence in participation; distributed cognition (Hutchins) spreads the "mind" of the project across people and artifacts; genres (Yates & Orlikowski) structure the recurrent communicative moves; Conway's Law warns that the group's communication structure will imprint itself on the artifact. Sensemaking (Weick) and narrative (Czarniawska) are how the collective converts equivocal events into shared, actionable accounts.

**(3) What dynamics come into play?** Planning versus emergence (Mintzberg & Waters; Sarasvathy); structure versus improvisation (Suchman; Feldman & Pentland); drift (technical debt as accreted drift; translation's betrayals; genre drift); bifurcation (effectual pivots on contingency; Schön's back-talk forcing reframing; wicked-problem re-formulation); and sensemaking as the connective tissue. The recurring meta-finding is dialectical recursion, not binary choice: order is continuously produced out of, and dissolved back into, action. The planning fallacy and "Goals Gone Wild" explain why the *planning* pole, over-relied upon, misfires — and why institutionalized emergence (agile, affordable loss, reference-class forecasting, reflective practice) outperforms both pure planning and pure improvisation.

## Recommendations
Although this review is theoretical, the following staged reading-and-reasoning path lets a practitioner or scholar operationalize it.

1. **Start with the ontology (why the gap is normal).** Read Tsoukas & Chia (2002) and Naur (1985) together; then Feldman & Pentland (2003). Benchmark: if you can restate your roadmap as an *ostensive* aspect that daily work legitimately rewrites, you have internalized the core move. If you still experience every deviation as failure, stay here.
2. **Adopt a decision logic matched to uncertainty.** Where outcomes are predictable, causal/goal-setting logic (Locke & Latham) is efficient; where they are genuinely uncertain, shift to effectuation (Sarasvathy) and emergent/umbrella strategy (Mintzberg & Waters). Threshold that should trigger the switch: when you cannot specify a stable goal or reliable base rate, stop optimizing toward a fixed target and start from means + affordable loss.
3. **Instrument the drift you cannot avoid.** Treat technical debt (Cunningham 1992) and Lehman's laws as accounting categories, not moral failures; make debt visible and schedule repayment. Benchmark: a maintained, explicit debt register and a bounded rate of change per release (conservation of familiarity).
4. **Protect the "theory of the program."** Per Naur, prioritize continuity of the people who hold the design theory over documentation completeness; onboarding = theory transfer, not document handover. Threshold to act: whenever the bus-factor for a subsystem approaches 1.
5. **Correct planning bias structurally.** Apply reference-class forecasting (Kahneman & Tversky) to estimates and watch for the "Goals Gone Wild" pathologies (metric-gaming, tunnel vision) whenever you tighten targets.
6. **Use artifacts as boundary objects, not contracts.** Design roadmaps, tickets, and specs to be re-interpretable across stakeholders (Star & Griesemer; Yates & Orlikowski), and expect the genre itself to drift.

What would change these recommendations: strong evidence that a given project is genuinely "tame" (stable requirements, reliable base rates, low coupling) would shift the balance back toward deliberate planning and goal-setting; conversely, rising coupling, personnel churn, or requirement volatility should push further toward effectual, iterative, theory-preserving practice.

## Caveats
- **Structural over-representation of large Western organizations.** The empirical bedrock — Berkeley's museum (Star & Griesemer), naval navigation (Hutchins), R&D and manufacturing firms (Nonaka; Feldman & Pentland), UK government-funded networks (Rethinking Project Management) — is overwhelmingly large, Western, institutionally resourced, and multi-actor. A telling illustration is Sampaio, Marinho & Moura's "Systematic Review on Project Actuality" (Federal University of Pernambuco, 2014, arXiv:1411.1945): of roughly 800 papers screened, only 14 final studies met inclusion criteria; most (13 studies) were from Northern Europe, most were conducted in medium-to-large organizations (so that "it became easy to exclude micro or small organizations"), and about one third were in the public sector.
- **Near-absence of solo work and very small structures.** Almost every framework presupposes multiple interacting agents (communities of practice, boundary objects, translation, Conway's Law, distributed cognition, temporary organizations). The individual managing several concurrent projects must reinterpret these as intrapersonal or tool-mediated dynamics — a legitimate but under-theorized move. This is a genuine gap, not merely a caveat.
- **Funding- and publication-driven bias.** What gets studied reflects what is fundable and visible: named, bounded, often high-budget projects. Failed, abandoned, informal, hobbyist, and maintenance-only work is under-published, biasing the corpus toward heroic conception and realization over quiet, long-run maintenance.
- **Contested and evolving constructs.** SECI is empirically contested (Gourlay 2006); goal-setting's universality is contested (Ordóñez et al. 2009); and Lehman's laws are only partially confirmed for modern and open-source contexts. Per Godfrey and colleagues' review of studies testing the laws ("On the Evolution of Lehman's Laws," University of Waterloo, 2013), Laws I (continuing change) and VI (continuing growth) "have been validated by all the studies," whereas Laws II (increasing complexity), IV (conservation of organizational stability), and V (conservation of familiarity) "have been invalidated for most of the cases." The technical-debt metaphor also has acknowledged limits (e.g., Kruchten, Nord & Ozkaya). These are live debates, not settled facts.
- **Verification note.** Author lists for the 2006 Rethinking Project Management special-issue papers differ (Winter, Smith, Morris & Cicmil for "Directions"; Winter, Smith, Cooke-Davies & Cicmil for the "process/story" companion); the Callon (1986) chapter page range is most authoritatively 196–233; Winograd & Flores (1986) was first published by Ablex (later reissued by Addison-Wesley); Schön (1983) is Basic Books (US)/Temple Smith (UK). These are reconciled in the text.
