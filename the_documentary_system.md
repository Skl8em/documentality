# The Documentary System

## Communication, Archive, and the Life Cycle of the Inscribed Act

*A companion to* The Inscribed Act: A Coordinate Theory of the Organizational Document *and* A Rhetoric of Organizational Genres

---

## Abstract

The parent theory locates a single document and defers two questions to a study of the *system* that carries it: how documents cohere and interoperate, and how they live, age, and die. This companion takes up both and reaches a claim the parent did not anticipate. The "documentary system" is not one system but **two**, held apart by two goals that no single arrangement can serve at once — to be found and used *now* by a reader, and to stand as evidence *over time*. Each goal is the founding problem of a different mature discipline: information architecture and librarianship on one side, archival science and records management on the other. Most organizational documentation is poor because it applies one discipline's apparatus to the other's problem. The two systems meet at a single seam — the distinction between the document that states a present **state** and the record that fixes a past **change** — and that seam also settles the parent's one unplaced coordinate, the temporal orientation of the act (§7), by showing it was never a coordinate of the act but a relation between two entities of the system. Both systems, moreover, are **recursive**: the corpus is a scale-relative, fractal structure — a single README at its base, deepening into sub-branches only as the organization it serves multiplies its teams, functions, and processes — so that where a document lives is fixed by the reach of what it settles. The study closes by revising the parent's suggested frame: the life of the record is better governed by the **records continuum** than by distributed cognition, which explains why externalization works but not what shape the externalized corpus takes.

---

## 1. One corpus, two masters

Every organizational corpus is pulled in two directions that no single arrangement satisfies. The parent essay treats "the system" as one object, to be studied under distributed cognition. On inspection it divides.

The first master is **communication**. A document must be found — by a reader who is often pressed and rarely an expert in the matter at hand — and, at its best, must let that reader stumble on what they did not know to look for. This is not cosmetic. An intelligible arrangement is the precondition of adoption; a corpus that cannot be navigated is a corpus that is worked around, and the writing in it, however correct, does nothing. The beneficiary is a present reader, and the horizon is now.

The second master is **evidence**. A document must also hold what was decided, when, by whom, and on what grounds, in a form that can be produced and trusted later — before an auditor, a successor, or the author two years on. This master is indifferent to browsing. It demands provenance, integrity, and endurance. The beneficiary is a future examiner, and the horizon is the long term.

These masters do not merely differ in emphasis; they command incompatible arrangements, because a good order for the one is a poor order for the other. The order that lets a reader browse by the shape of their need scatters the evidence of an activity across the tree; the order that keeps the evidence of an activity intact buries what a reader came to find. This is why "organize the documentation" is not one task with one right answer. It is two tasks, each the founding problem of a mature discipline that the other discipline's practitioners tend not to read: **information architecture and librarianship** for communication, **archival science and records management** for evidence. Call the two the **communication system** and the **archival system**. The root failure of most corpora is the conflation of the two — importing the archive's control apparatus into the reader's surface, or letting the reader's surface stand in for the archive's memory.

The rest of this essay develops each system on its own terms, locates the seam that joins them, and shows that the parent's deferred problem lives on that seam.

## 2. What belongs together: provenance and its limit

The archival system answers a question the reader's intuition gets wrong. Asked *which documents belong together*, intuition says: those about the same subject. The archival answer is different and better: those that are traces of the same **activity**. A request for change, the decision it triggers, the test that validates it, and the account that reports on it belong together not because they share a topic but because they share a **provenance** — they are the residue of one chain of action, and their juxtaposition is itself evidence. Subject-based filing fails because a single document serves several subjects and must then be duplicated or arbitrarily shelved where half its readers will never look; provenance-based grouping does not, because a document has one activity that produced it even when it bears on many topics.

Provenance has a famous limit, and naming it is essential because it is exactly the limit a large, multi-speed project hits. A rigid rule of *one activity, one folder* does not survive scale. The entities that produce documents — teams, functions, projects — split, merge, and dissolve, while the documents outlive them. In 1966 the archivist Peter Scott drew the conclusion in a paper whose title states it plainly, *The Record Group Concept: A Case for Abandonment*: the assumption that a body of records maps one-to-one onto a single stable creator is false, and a system built on it must re-file the world at every reorganization. His replacement, the Australian *series system*, rests on a move that clarifies the whole subject: **separate the description of the records from the description of their context**, and relate the two by many, dated links rather than fusing them into one hierarchy.

From that move follows the distinction that makes provenance operable: **function** versus **project**. A function is a permanent responsibility of the organization — risk management, regulatory reporting, a standing calculation. It does not end. A project is bounded — a migration, a compliance effort — and it closes. The distinction dissolves the puzzles that make provenance seem unworkable at scale. A change that touches three functions at once need not be filed three times: its provenance is the project that produced it, and its bearing on the functions is a *relation*, not a location. A migration that ends while what it set in motion continues is no paradox: the project closes and its record freezes, while the function it served — or an operations activity that inherits its outputs — carries on. A system in pure maintenance is not without provenance; its active provenance is operation, not the construction that has concluded. What looked like a defeat for provenance was only the defeat of provenance imagined as a single tree.

## 3. Shelf and catalogue: why provenance cannot arrange the reading surface

The apparatus of the archival system is built for control, not for reading, and applying it to the communication system is the second great conflation. The series system is powerful precisely because it decouples the arrangement of records from any browsable order — which is tolerable in an archive, where a trained mediator and a finding aid stand between the holdings and the enquirer. A communication system has no mediator. It has a reader who opens a page tree, and for that reader many-to-many relations are not navigation; they are a puzzle. A corpus organized only by cross-cutting metadata — every document tagged, the reader left to combine the tags — sounds elegant and is unusable, because ordinary readers do not think in faceted queries.

Librarianship resolved this long ago by refusing to pick one device and instead separating two. The **shelf** gives each item one place, and one only; its order is chosen to make *browsing* possible, so that a reader looking for one thing meets its neighbours and finds what they were not seeking. The **catalogue** gives many points of access to the same item — by author, subject, keyword — and serves the reader who already knows what they want or is crossing criteria. The decisive fact is that both are needed and neither substitutes for the other. A catalogue without a shelf destroys serendipity; a shelf without a catalogue forbids cross-cutting retrieval. The reading surface therefore needs a shelf — **one intelligible tree** through which readers actually navigate — and a catalogue layered over it. Metadata belongs to the catalogue, and its proper use is to *generate* readable index surfaces, not to serve as the reader's interface: the reader browses hubs written for them while the machine, and only the machine, reads the tags.

Because a shelf admits one order, the whole difficulty concentrates in choosing its axis, and the choice is governed by a principle classification theory calls **warrant**: the order must fit the mental map of those who search. Different communities do not search alike — a maintainer looks by component, an analyst by business function, an examiner by obligation — and one tree cannot serve them equally. Choosing the axis is therefore choosing the **dominant community** the surface exists to serve. For a corpus whose first purpose is to move information in the shared language of the business, that axis is the functions of the business, not the components of the implementation; where the dominant readers are builders, it inverts. The choice is not neutral, and pretending otherwise is how reading surfaces become unusable for everyone in the name of serving all.

This is also where the two systems part most sharply, and naming the difference sharpens both. Each organizes by "function," but not the same function. The archival system orders by the **producer's** activities — the provenance of §2 — and because those activities are carried out by teams and units, the archival tree comes to mirror the producing organization. That mirroring is not an archival curiosity: it is the same law the software world knows as **Conway's** — that the structure a group produces copies the structure of its communication — now tested and named the *mirroring hypothesis* in the study of modular design. Respect des fonds and Conway's law are one observation reached from two disciplines. The reading surface, by contrast, orders by the **functions served** to the consumer — what the reader wants to know or do. Both mirror "function"; the archive mirrors the producer's, the surface the reader's, and confusing the two is how an archive gets shelved for browsing or a reading surface gets filed by team.

Rigor forces one correction on the word *organization*. What the archive should mirror is the organization's **ways of working** — its functions and activities, which persist — not its **org chart**, which does not; this is the ISO 15489 lesson already met in §2, and Scott's, that units churn while functions endure. Mirror the stable verbs, in other words, and let the volatile boxes hang off them as changing relations. And a warning rides with Conway: the law is descriptive, so an archive left to itself will mirror whatever organization exists, including a bad one — the discipline of *designing* the mirror rather than suffering it is what the software world calls the inverse-Conway move, and its documentary form is the refusal to fossilize a broken structure in the record. That the axis is a choice, and a choice that privileges some searchers while it pushes others to the catalogue's margins, is not a defect to be smoothed over but a fact to be owned: every classification, as Bowker and Star showed, carries its residual categories and its silences. Choosing the dominant community is an exercise of power, and the honest system makes the choice visible — declared, and answerable — rather than presenting its tree as natural.

## 4. Scale and recursion: how both trees deepen

The two organizing principles just set out — provenance for the archive, the shelf for the reader — share a property neither section stated: they are **recursive**, and the depth to which they recurse is fixed by the complexity of the organization they serve, not chosen in advance. A documentary system is not a structure of a settled size but a fractal one, and forgetting this produces two opposite failures — the elaborate apparatus imposed on a corpus too small to need it, and the flat corpus that has long outgrown its single tree.

The degree zero is a single README. One person, one activity, one reader who is also the writer: there is nothing to separate, and the whole system is one node. This is not a primitive case to be apologized for; it is the fractal's base — the system with a single module — and many attempts to "set up documentation properly" fail by starting three scales too high. A scale is added only when something multiplies: a second team, a distinct function, a process that outlives its author. Each addition is a *subdivision*, not a new summit; the corpus grows downward into branches, and what was the whole becomes a node within a larger whole.

The law of that subdivision is old and has been found independently in several disciplines, which is the sign that it is real rather than merely elegant. Herbert Simon called it **quasi-decomposability**: a complex system takes the form of a hierarchy of sub-systems in which the interactions *inside* a module are stronger than those *between* modules (*The Architecture of Complexity*, 1962). Translated to the corpus: a decision that touches only one sub-system belongs to that sub-system — it is weakly coupled to the rest — and the center need retain only what crosses modules. David Parnas gave the operative rule its name, **information hiding**: a module is drawn around a decision precisely so that the rest of the system need not know it (1972); read as documentation, the center keeps the interfaces and not the decisions hidden behind them. Stafford Beer gave the same shape a governance form in the **Viable System Model** — the principle of recursion, that every viable system contains viable systems, each level replicating the same structure of operating and regulating; read as a corpus, each sub-branch carries its own equivalent of a decision record, and only what concerns the whole rises. And the shape appears once more as a doctrine of *decision* — **subsidiarity**, that a matter is settled at the lowest competent level — which is the normative version of the descriptive fact, and is best used here descriptively, its political freight left at the door.

What fixes the level at which a document lives is the reach of what it settles, and here the parent's own vocabulary already supplies the measure. Elliott Jaques' **time-span of discretion** — the horizon over which a decision commits — sorts documents by exactly the axis the parent calls *horizon* and this study carries into the two systems: a long-horizon, wide-reach decision lives at the center; a short-horizon, narrow-reach one lives in a local sub-branch, accessible to those it concerns and unobtrusive to those it does not. Why the tree must deepen at all, rather than the center simply absorbing more, is answered by Ashby's law of **requisite variety**: a regulator can only govern the variety it can match, so past a threshold the center cannot hold the whole and must devolve — the added scale is not bureaucratic accretion but the only structure that keeps a growing system governable. In the language of the builders who will use this most, the same law is the **bounded context** of domain-driven design: each context owns its model and its terms, and what passes between contexts passes through an explicit contract rather than a shared interior.

One caution keeps this from contradicting §2. The fractal is the *ideal* shelf — clean subdivision, weak coupling, a decision that falls into exactly one branch. Real corpora do not fully decompose; that is precisely why the archival system needed the series system and its many, dated links. The recursion is therefore the default, not the whole story: it arranges what decomposes, while the series system's relations carry the residue that refuses to — the change that genuinely touches three functions at once, the concern that cuts across the tree. A mature system runs both: a recursive spine deep enough to match the organization's complexity, and a thin layer of cross-cutting links for what the spine cannot hold. The error is not having a residue; it is either denying it — forcing a clean tree onto a coupled world — or drowning in it, dissolving the tree into the pure network that is the unusable all-metadata surface of §3.

## 5. The life of the act: trajectory, not decline

The parent essay predicts that a document's coordinates engender its trajectory, and the prediction holds: a record that *decides* is frozen and archived; one that *commits* is re-issued in versions; one that *describes* is maintained; one that *proves* is re-run against preserved inputs (the forces and their verbs are developed in *A Rhetoric of Organizational Genres*). The life cycle is not an afterlife appended to the act; it is the act's coordinates read forward in time. Two rival models give that reading its shape, and the choice between them is not idle.

The **records life-cycle** model, inherited from Schellenberg, describes ordered phases: creation, active use, a semi-active middle age, and a final disposition into destruction or permanent archive. It is linear, and value migrates down it from the creator's immediate use to a later, secondary use by others. The **records continuum**, from the Australian school, denies the discrete phases and the end state alike: a record is never finished, never merely inactive, always liable to be drawn into a new use by a new actor. The two are usually best held as complementary lenses rather than rivals, but the continuum earns a stronger role here, because it is the systemic twin of the parent's central complication. What the parent calls **re-inscription** (§6) — a reading that activates a document from a position other than the one it was written from — is, at the scale of the system, what the continuum calls *pluralisation*: the record organized into corporate memory can always be pulled into a wider arena and read by a reader it was not written for. The parent's observation about single documents is the continuum's observation about the corpus. The mechanism of that pulling has its own name in the sociology of inscription: Latour's **immutable mobile** — a record that travels without deforming, accumulating in the centres of calculation that read it. The archive's evidential power and the continuum's pluralisation are two faces of what immutable mobiles make possible: because the inscription holds its form as it moves, it can be carried into an arena its author never saw and still be read as the same record.

The controversy between the two models even reproduces the parent's own tension between the *assumed mono-positional* and the *suffered re-positionable* document (§6). Against the continuum, Duranti and MacNeil hold that a record's context settles — that the archival bond binding it to its action reaches stability when the action concludes. That is the mono-positional stance at the level of the system: some records are written to settle and do. The continuum is the re-positionable stance: none finally settles. The debate is thirty years old and unresolved, which is the honest status of the matter; a mature practice does not resolve it in the abstract but *chooses*, for a given body of records, where on that spectrum it should sit.

Digital preservation supplies the last piece and returns the recipient to view. The OAIS reference model recasts the life cycle not as decline but as a transformation of **packages**: the same intellectual content exists as what a producer submits, as what an archive preserves with its provenance and fixity, and as what is disseminated to a requester. The content is one; its packaging is set by where it stands in its life and toward whom it moves. And the model's *Designated Community* — the readership presumed able to understand the preserved content unaided — is the parent's **distance** coordinate returned as a preservation parameter: one preserves *for* a named community, and that naming fixes how much context must travel with the content for it to remain intelligible when its writers are gone.

## 6. State and change: the deferred coordinate, relocated

The parent leaves one dimension unplaced — the temporal orientation of the act, whether a document fixes a state or records a change — and suspects it lies between an axis and a property of the life cycle (§7). It lies in neither, because it is not a coordinate of the act at all. It is a **relation between two entities of the system**, and once seen that way it is not hard but merely double.

Almost every documentary domain runs two artifacts in parallel: the document that states the present **state** ("here is how this works now") and the record that fixes a **change** ("here is what was altered, and why"). The clearest instance is legal. In French practice the act that amends a law is published in the *Journal officiel*: it is authentic and juridically opposable. The *consolidated version* — the text as it now reads, all amendments folded in — carries informative value but no legal force; it is a reconstruction, derived from the sequence of amending acts, and it is fallible, as the occasional silent error in consolidation demonstrates. The current state is the convenience; the register of changes is what holds. The same duality structures a technical corpus. The record of a change — a decision record, a migration step — is **diachronic**: it belongs to the project that produced it, it is not rewritten, it freezes. (The parent's *Rhetoric* marks the same property from the writer's side: a decision is dated-fixed, and a superseding decision is a new record, never an edit.) The description of a state — the reference, the account of how the thing works — is **synchronic**: it belongs to the function and is kept current. A change *updates* the state document and is *filed with* its project. This duality is now native to software architecture, which gives the parent's audience its most concrete instance: in the pattern called **event sourcing**, the append-only log of events is the authoritative record of change — diachronic, never rewritten — while the current state is a **projection** derived from that log, rebuildable and, like a consolidated statute, fallible; a version-control history and its working tree stand in the same relation. The engineering practice and the *Journal officiel* enact one structure. The two faces are joined but do not share a place, a life span, or a service.

This also corrects an intuition the parent entertains and rightly distrusts — that state and change might relate as an integral to its derivative. The intuition holds in one direction and fails in the other, and naming the asymmetry names the whole problem. **Backward**, it holds: the present consolidated state *is* the integral of a closed sequence of past changes, and can in principle be re-derived from them. **Forward**, it fails: the future does not accumulate. A document weighing three possible courses is not the derivative of anything; it is a *proposal* over open futures, a different force entirely. State integrates over a settled past; it cannot integrate a future that is still plural. Four independent traditions model the backward relation in the same shape — the bibliographic (two expressions of one work), the diplomatic (an authoritative original and a derived copy of lower authority), the legal (the opposable enactment and the informative consolidation), and the computational (the event log and the state folded from it) — and their convergence, across archives and engineering alike, is the evidence that the relation is real and the placement correct.

The seam matters beyond the parent's §7, because it is where the two systems join. The **same content** lives in the communication system as maintained state — the synchronic surface a reader browses — and in the archival system as frozen change — the diachronic record an examiner reproduces. Neither system owns the content; each holds one face of it. Such doubly-held content — robust enough to keep a single identity across two communities, plastic enough to serve each in its own form — is what Star and Griesemer called a **boundary object**, and the discipline of keeping the derived state honest to its authoritative changes is what keeps this boundary object from splitting into two divergent records.

## 7. Two systems, two disciplines

The model can now be stated as one picture. The documentary system the parent deferred is two systems, each governed by its own discipline, joined to the other at the state–change seam, and deepening together by the recursive law of §4.

| | Communication system | Archival system |
|---|---|---|
| **Serves** | a present reader | a future examiner |
| **Horizon** | now | the long term |
| **Optimizes for** | findability, browsing, adoption | provenance, integrity, endurance |
| **Dominant form** | present state (**synchronic**) | record of change (**diachronic**) |
| **Organizing principle** | one intelligible tree, plus generated indexes | the producing activity (provenance) |
| **Mirrors** | the functions served to the reader | the producer's ways of working |
| **Governing discipline** | information architecture, librarianship | archival science, records management |
| **Central question** | *how does one navigate without already knowing?* | *what holds as evidence, and for how long?* |

The picture forces a revision of the parent's suggestion that the life-cycle study belongs to distributed cognition. Distributed cognition explains *why* externalizing knowledge into artifacts works — availability, trust, shared access — but it is a theory of cognition spread across actors *at a time*, and the deferred subject is the corpus *through time and across actors*. That is the continuum's subject exactly. It is also the subject that organization theory studies as **organizational memory** (Walsh and Ungson, 1991): the stores, and the re-use across time and personnel, by which a firm remembers past decisions — a management-science twin that corroborates, from outside archival science, the move to make the continuum rather than distributed cognition the governing frame. So the governing frame of the archival system and the life it carries is the **records continuum**, with distributed cognition as its complement: the continuum gives the shape of the system that externalization produces, distributed cognition the account of why the production pays. And provenance, so central above, takes its proper place: it is the right principle for the archival system and the wrong one for the communication system, which is governed instead by the shelf-and-catalogue discipline of information architecture. Provenance was never wrong; it was answered to the wrong system.

## 8. What the model predicts, and what it cannot yet say

Held to the parent's methodological discipline — that a typology should confess whether it is *closed* or merely *saturated* — the model earns some predictions and must leave some questions open.

It predicts the commonest failure of real corpora, and locates its cause precisely. The wiki that accumulates into undifferentiated sediment, named but not diagnosed in the parent essay (§9), is here a system missing three separable things at once: a provenance that says which activity each document is a trace of, a retention rule that says when each document dies, and a reader's tree chosen for a real community. The three lacks are independent and are repaired independently, which is a more useful diagnosis than "the wiki is a mess." It predicts, too, where cost is owed and where it is not — the same economy the parent draws for *armor* and *scaffolding* applies to preservation: one preserves *for* a designated community and *against* a horizon, and paying archival cost on documents whose life is a sprint is the system-level form of over-armoring. The same economy has an upstream limit the model marks but does not chart: the knowledge-management literature on tacit knowledge (Nonaka; Hansen, Nohria, and Tierney) holds that some of what an organization knows resists codification and is dulled by forcing it into a document — so the model governs what is written without settling where writing should stop.

It leaves three questions open, and marks them as open. The status of the life-cycle and continuum models is unsettled, and this study treats them as two lenses rather than crowning a winner. The modeling of documents that *accrete* — the living log, the integrating resource — is genuinely hard, and not only here: cataloguing met the same difficulty in extending its bibliographic model to serials, and only partly tamed it, which is reason to expect no clean closure for the "current head" of a perpetually updated document. And the two-system division itself is offered as *saturated*, not *closed*: it is the split that keeps recurring until new material stops adding to it, not a proof that a third system cannot appear. Like the parent, this is a modern, bureaucratic, broadly Western account of the record, and it does not claim to travel across all cultures of the archive.

---

## Coda

The parent theory locates the act; the *Rhetoric* writes it; this study carries it — through the corpus that must be browsed today and the archive that must be proved tomorrow. The three make one claim with three faces: that what a document *is*, how it should be *written*, and how it must be *kept* are the same act met at three moments and by three disciplines. The single insight of the present face is that "keeping" is itself double, because a corpus answers to two masters who cannot be served by one order — a reader in the present and an examiner in the future — and that the two are joined at a single seam, where the record of a change becomes the statement of a state, and grow together by a single recursive law, a README that branches only as far as its organization compels. Name the systems apart, govern each by its own discipline, and keep the derived state honest to its authoritative changes: that is the whole of it, and the points the model cannot yet name still have a place within it.
