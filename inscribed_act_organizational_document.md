# The Inscribed Act
## A Coordinate Theory of the Organizational Document

*A conceptual essay*

---

## Abstract

What is an organizational document, and what does it do? Organizations write far more than the material a person consults to learn or operate a system — they write decisions, commitments, proofs, proposals, accounts, conventions, mandates, writing whose purpose is not to inform a user but to regulate relationships between actors across social and temporal distance. This essay asks what all of that writing *is*. It draws together the several mature disciplines that each describe, from one angle, what writing does inside human collectives, and integrates them into a single coordinate system in which any organizational document can be located.

This essay reframes the organizational document not as a container of information classified by the reader's need, but as a **typified communicative act, inscribed from a definite position, that performs an illocutionary function toward a definite recipient**. To inscribe is not incidental to the act: it is the mode by which the act becomes a durable social object (Ferraris's *documentality*: object = inscribed act). Two axes locate what the act does. The first — **illocutionary force** — is anchored in the philosophy of language; it admits a principled closure, but only at a coarse grain. The second — **perlocutionary intention** — is the uptake the act seeks in the reader, and it is *largely independent* of the first: no force is welded to a given intention. This second axis has no neutral degree, for every inscription, merely by existing, poses what it says as the world and silences what it omits. The named "forces" a field recognizes — explain, prove, teach, justify — are frequent **conjunctions** of these two axes, reified into genres, not primitives. A third structure, **the recipient relation**, is irreducibly twofold, combining *distance* (how much shared code the reader is assumed to possess) and *power* (a graded, many-formed relation of sanction, not a binary); it is an open, empirically saturated typology, not a closed one.

The essay then argues that a document's coordinates are fixed at writing but can be re-activated — re-inscribed — by reading, which is the source of a structurally irreducible class of documentary stress; that a good coordinate label is not merely classificatory but **generative**, telling the writer what matters; and that one apparent dimension — the temporal orientation of the act — does not defer but **dissolves**, part absorbed by force, part a property of content, and the remainder relocated to a companion study of the documentary *system*. Established use-centered frameworks appear, in this light, as *projections* of the coordinate space — regions it maps well — which the account recovers rather than replaces. Throughout, the methodological claim is that most typologies in this field are legitimately *saturated* rather than *closed*, and that honesty about which is which is worth more than a false claim of completeness.

## 1. What organizations write

Organizations write a great deal, and only a fraction of it is addressed to someone learning or operating a system. Alongside the tutorial and the reference sit the record of an architectural decision, the minutes of a governing committee, a quarterly status report, an internal naming convention, a service-level promise to a customer, the requirements a project receives from its sponsors and the requirements it issues to its own suppliers. These are documents in the full sense, and they do something other than inform a user: they decide, commit, prove, propose, mandate, account. Their purpose is to regulate relationships between actors — across the organization, toward the outside world, and toward the future.

To understand them, the useful question is not *what does the reader need to know?* but **what is this document doing, and toward whom?** The first question already has a mature answer: use-centered documentation frameworks — Diátaxis the most refined among them — organize the writing that teaches or assists a user, and within that region they are the right instrument. Their coordinates are coordinates of that user's need, and nothing in what follows displaces them there.

The aim of this essay is to find the larger space of which that region is a part — the space that also holds the decision, the proof, and the mandate — and to give it a coordinate system as principled as the one the frameworks gave their corner. Seen from that space, a document is not a container of information but an act performed toward a recipient; and the established frameworks reappear, without being set aside, as one of its regions (§4.2, §9).

## 2. Reframing: the document as an inscribed act

The proposed reorientation is compact:

> An organizational document is a typified communicative act, inscribed from a definite position, that performs an illocutionary function toward a definite recipient. Its informational content is the medium of the act, not its essence.

Two consequences follow immediately. First, the unit of analysis is the *act*, not the *text*. This does not set the two against each other. Inscription is precisely the mode by which an act becomes a durable social object — this is the point of Ferraris's *documentality*, whose constitutive law, *object = inscribed act* (where *inscribed* means *recorded*), is offered against Searle's grounding of institutional facts in collective intentionality: what makes a decision, a debt, or a commitment exist socially is not that a group holds it in mind but that it is recorded. The text, then, is not a rival to the act; it is the act made to last and to travel. What reading can later do (§6) is not to turn the text into a different thing but to *take it up* into a different act. Second, a document has a **position of inscription** — a set of coordinates fixed at the moment of writing — which determines the form and the codes the document must adopt to be intelligible. A document is intelligible when its form and substance are in their place; a document optimized for every possible position is in its place in none.

There is a third consequence, easy to miss and central to the practical value of the theory. The coordinates are not merely descriptive labels applied after the fact; they are **generative**. To know a document's force is to know what matters in it and how to write it. A short illustration: the conventional `README` of a software project is reflexively classed as a *description* of the project, and writing it under that label tends to produce an indifferent inventory of features. But its true function is to **orient** — to situate the reader, answer *what is this and why does it concern me*, and route them onward. Named correctly, the same file is written better, because the label tells the writer where the centre of gravity lies. The coordinate is an instruction, not a bucket. This generative property is the bridge from the present theory to its operational companion, *A Rhetoric of Organizational Genres*, which develops the move structures that each force implies.

The remainder of this essay specifies the coordinates of the position of inscription, then complicates the picture by observing that reading can re-inscribe a document into a position other than the one it was written from.

## 3. Disciplinary foundations

The theory does not invent its categories; it synthesizes several mature bodies of thought, each of which describes, from one angle, what writing does inside human collectives.

- **Philosophy of language** (Austin; Searle; Anscombe). Language does not only describe the world, it acts upon it; and the ways it can act partition, at a coarse grain, by *direction of fit* — whether words are made to match the world, the world is to be made to match the words, both, or neither. Austin also separates the **illocutionary** act (what is done in saying something) from the **perlocutionary** effect (what is produced in the hearer — being convinced, oriented, taught). This pair grounds the two axes of §4.

- **Social ontology of the document** (Ferraris; and, for inscription as a traveling form, Latour). Ferraris's *documentality* makes recording constitutive of social reality — *object = inscribed act* — proposed explicitly against Searle's collective intentionality, and able where Searle struggles to account for "negative" entities such as debts and for the intangible objects of the Web. This is why the unit is the *inscribed* act and not merely the speech act. Latour's **immutable mobile** supplies the complement: an inscription holds its form as it moves, and so can be carried into positions its author never saw — the precondition of re-inscription (§6).

- **Documentation science** (Otlet; Briet; Buckland). The field whose founding question is *what is a document?* Briet's answer — a document is evidence in support of a fact, any sign preserved or recorded in order to represent or demonstrate a phenomenon — places the *evidential* function at the center and frees the concept from textual form, admitting diagrams, datasets, and slides as documents in the full sense.

- **Sociology of boundary objects** (Star & Griesemer). Artifacts can be plastic enough to serve several communities' local needs while robust enough to keep a common identity across them. A document *built* for several communities at once can hold unlike groups in coordination without prior agreement — the mechanism that makes the *declared* multi-positional document (§6) possible.

- **Organizational and rhetorical genre theory** (Miller; Yates & Orlikowski; Bakhtin). A genre is a typified communicative action taken in response to a recurrent situation, recognizable by its form — Miller's formula, which Yates and Orlikowski carry into organizations. Genres travel in *systems* — proposal leads to decision leads to account — so that no document is fully intelligible in isolation. Bakhtin's *addressivity* adds that every utterance is shaped by the response it anticipates: the recipient is, in part, built into the act (§5.4).

- **Distributed and extended cognition** (Hutchins; Clark & Chalmers). Cognition is distributed across people and artifacts; reasoning is *sedimented* into tools so that others need not redo it. The conditions under which an external store functions as memory — availability, trust, accessibility, endorsement — double as a specification of documentary quality. The unit here is the *system*, not the single artifact, which is why this foundation governs the companion study of the documentary system more than the present one.

- **Genre linguistics** (Swales). A text decomposes into *moves*, each performing a coherent communicative function, legible only to the *discourse community* trained in the genre. This grounds the *distance* component of the recipient relation, and supplies the analytic unit — the move — on which the operational companion is built.

- **The anthropology of bureaucratic writing** (Weber; Riles; Hull). The file is the material basis of legal-rational authority; records carry power and assign accountability. This grounds the *power* component of the recipient relation and explains the frictions any documentation practice meets (§10).

## 4. The first axis: illocutionary force

### 4.1 A principled closure, at a coarse grain

The first axis answers: *what does the document do?* Its value is that, uniquely in this theory, it admits a closure that does not rest on the author's own arbitrary partitioning. The closure comes from outside, from the notion of **direction of fit**. An utterance either makes its words answer to the world, or asks the world to answer to its words, or does both at once, or neither:

| Direction of fit | The act… | Temporal orientation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Word-to-world | makes words match how things are | present / past |
| World-to-word | asks the world to be made to match the words | future |
| Both at once | changes reality by being issued | the present instant of issue |
| Neither | expresses a state without fitting either way | — |

These four are not a partition the author chose; they are exhaustive over the logically possible relations between words and world. This is the only genuinely closed structure in the theory — and even it is contested: the philosophical literature has disputed for decades whether expressives and declarations fit the scheme cleanly. Closure here means *deductive*, not *uncontroversial*. And it is closure of the *schema*: a single real document may instantiate several forces at once, and what to do about that is taken up in §4.3.

A compact gloss helps carry these families, so long as it is read as a gloss and not a redefinition. In the organizational setting the three populated families are three registers: word-to-world is the register of **knowing** — representing what is the case; world-to-word the register of **doing** — bringing something about; and the both-at-once of declaration the register of **governing** — instituting a fact by the authority of the record. The fourth family, *neither* (the expressive), is organizationally thin and falls away, so *know / do / govern* is a shorthand over the three active families, not the closure — which remains the four directions of fit — and *govern* names a marked, self-guaranteeing category, not a third primitive symmetric with the others. Read with that care, the gloss states this essay's contribution in three words: use-centered frameworks map *knowing* and *doing* — reference and explanation, how-to and tutorial — and the region they do not reach is *governing*, where the decision, the mandate, and the commitment live. It is no accident that the companion study calls the archival half of the system *govern and record*: the act that governs is the one that produces the record to be kept.

### 4.2 The second axis: perlocutionary intention, and its lack of a zero

A second axis cuts across force, and the two are — this matters — **largely independent**. Force is what the act does in relation to the world; the second axis is what the act intends to produce *in the reader*. Following Austin, the first is illocutionary, the second perlocutionary; and because the coordinates are fixed at writing, where only aims and not effects are available, the axis is one of perlocutionary **intention**, not effect. (The effect belongs to reading, and is treated as re-inscription in §6.)

The independence is near-total: an assertion may intend nothing beyond stating, or may intend to build the reader's understanding, or to win the reader's assent; a directive may serve a task or aim to form a competence. One must therefore *not* weld a given force to a given intention — to do so is a trap that no evidence supports. What can be said honestly is weaker and truer: some conjunctions are frequent, recognized, and reified into genres, and for a nameable reason. An intention often presupposes a kind of content: to be formative *about how a thing works*, one must assert about that thing, so the formative-epistemic aim *leans* toward the assertive force — not by rule, but because remodeling a reader's map requires propositional content to remodel it with. The pull is a corollary of what the aim needs, never a fixed pairing.

The axis has no neutral degree, and this is the correction that matters most. It is tempting to call its lowest value "served — the reader is left unchanged." No inscription leaves the reader unchanged. To write *this is how it works* is to pose the described world as *the* world and to make the undescribed invisible; to write *do it this way* is to carry the weight of the established and the customary. This is the **constitutive floor** of any inscription — what Ferraris makes ontological (to record is to make a social object exist) and what Bowker and Star make political (every classification naturalizes what it includes and silences what it omits). The floor is not chosen and cannot be escaped; it is the writer's power, exercised in the very act of selecting what to record. Above this floor sit the *intended* uptakes the writer may add:

- **formative** — to change what the reader knows or can do: reshape a conceptual model (**explain**) or a cognitive map (**orient**), or build a competence (**teach**);
- **suasive** — to change what the reader believes or wills: win assent to a claim (**prove**) or to a course (**propose**, **justify**);
- and, weakly attested, **affective** — to change how the reader feels (reassure, motivate), which mostly rides on the suasive and earns no rank of its own.

"Serving" the reader, then, names the case where the writer intends *no uptake beyond the floor* — never a case where the reader is untouched.

Two refinements within the formative aim matter for writing. **Explain** reshapes the reader's *conceptual model* — how and why a thing works. **Orient** reshapes the reader's *cognitive map* — where they are, what surrounds them, where to go next. A use-centered scheme with no cell for orientation is why overview pages and `README` files sit awkwardly in it; the present scheme names the function directly.

Crossing the *formative* value with direction of fit re-derives the two axes of Diátaxis — but now as a **projection**, and the word is exact. Diátaxis's *acquisition / application* axis is the formative aim; its *action / cognition* axis is the direction of fit; its four quadrants — explanation, tutorial, reference, how-to — fall out of the crossing. What the projection drops is the rest of the perlocutionary axis: the **suasive** aim (which is why Diátaxis has no room for a proposal or a decision — precisely the writing §1 opened with) and the **constitutive floor** (which is why Diátaxis can present a reference as neutral service). That a use-centered framework, built bottom-up from practice, lands exactly on this projection is still corroboration that the dimensions are real; naming it a projection is what keeps the corroboration honest.

### 4.3 Recognized cells, not a repertoire of primitives

Because the two axes are independent, the "forces" organizations actually name are not a flat list of primitives; several are **conjunctions** — a direction of fit crossed with a perlocutionary intention, reified into a genre because the situation recurs. *Describe* is near-primitive (assert, floor only); *explain* is assert × formative; *orient* is assert × formative-of-the-map; *prove* is assert × suasive; *teach* is direct × formative; *justify* is assert × suasive. Listing them side by side as if coordinate is the flattening §4.2 warns against. The table below is therefore a catalogue of **recognized cells** in the act-space, not a partition of forces — the intention shown is the one that defines the reified genre, not a necessary pairing:

| Illocutionary base | Typical intention | Recognized cell | The act… | Genres |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Word-to-world | floor only | **Describe** | states how things are | reference, data dictionary, API docs |
| Word-to-world | formative (map) | **Orient** | situates the reader in a whole | README, overview, landing page |
| Word-to-world | formative (model) | **Explain** | builds the reader's model of *why* | explanation, design rationale |
| Word-to-world | suasive | **Prove** | wins assent that a claim holds | audit report, test report, dossier |
| Word-to-world | floor / mild suasive | **Account** | reports what occurred vs. expectation | status report, postmortem, changelog |
| World-to-word | floor + custom | **Instruct** | tells the reader *how* to act | how-to, runbook, install guide |
| World-to-word | formative | **Teach** | builds a competence in the reader | tutorial, onboarding, workshop |
| World-to-word (declarative-leaning) | by authority | **Mandate** | institutes a norm to be obeyed | policy, convention, standard |
| Commissive | floor | **Commit** | binds the writer to a future | SLA, charter, contract |
| World-to-word | suasive | **Propose** | opens a future for deliberation | RFC, requirements, design proposal |
| Both | by record | **Decide** | institutes a choice by record | decision record, minutes, sign-off |

Three cautions travel with the table. It is empirically **saturated**, not closed: new cells appear as new situations recur. The pairings are **typical, not necessary**: any base can carry any intention, and the density of a cell reflects how often its situation arises, not a law — the aim of the coordinate space is to be able to *locate* any act, never to enumerate them all. And a single document may instantiate **several coordinates at once, along either axis**. This is tolerable while the document is small, and it is paid in illegibility as it grows: a document under too many coordinates becomes hard to place, for the system and for the reader alike. The remedy is the same recursive move the companion applies to scale — decompose along the seams — but the seams are of two orders.

**First order, by force.** The closed families give the crispest seams, and the fragments differ in *trajectory*: a design doc that describes the current system, proposes a change, and records the decision holds three forces whose lives diverge — the description is maintained, the proposal is deliberated and closed, the decision freezes and is archived — so splitting them is not tidiness but the recognition that they no longer live together. **Second order, and within a force, by perlocution.** When a single cell tries at once to serve-or-form and to persuade — an explanation that both builds the reader's model *and* argues the design was right — the finer split separates the formative reference from the suasive rationale. This cut is second-order because it changes nothing about what the act does to the *world*, only what it seeks in the *reader*; and it reaches only the **intended** layers — the constitutive floor is indivisible and rides on every fragment, however finely one splits. Force seams are deductively crisp, perlocutionary seams saturated; both are principled enough to split along, and one splits cleanly only along principled seams. Multiplicity of *recipient* is the one tension not always resolved by cutting: a document built for several positions at once can be held together as a boundary object (§6) rather than split.

A note on two common conflations. *Instruct* and *mandate* are often merged under "directive," but they differ in object: instruction governs the **how** of an already-settled task, whereas mandate settles the **what** — and mandate, by instituting an obligation, leans toward the declarative. The generative names that best guide writing (a decision record is better written as *justify*, a proposal as *argue*) are developed in the operational companion, which treats each cell as a rhetorical stance with its own move structure.

## 5. The second structure: the recipient relation

The recipient relation answers: *toward whom is the act performed?* It is, deliberately, **two coordinates carried under one heading**, because the heading names a single relation while the relation has two independent components.

### 5.1 Distance and power are orthogonal

The first component, **distance**, is the question of shared code: how much of the writer's discourse community the reader belongs to, and therefore how much can be presupposed and how much must be made explicit. The second, **power**, is the question of sanction: whether, and how, the reader can impose consequences on the writer. These vary independently:

| | Reader has little power over the writer | Reader has power over the writer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Shares the writer's code** | a peer on the same team (rough working note) | the writer's own manager (self-appraisal) |
| **Does not share the code** | an engineer at another firm (interface spec) | an auditor, a tax authority, an inspectorate (evidential dossier) |

Power is not the black-and-white of *has sanction / has none* but a **graded, many-formed** relation: the writer answers differently to a subordinate, a peer, a manager, a distant authority, and each setting rewrites how defensible, self-contained, and evidential the document must be — and even how one explains a thing's use, which is pitched down or up with the reader's standing. The 2×2 above *samples* the space; it does not partition it. There are as many postures as there are configurations of power.

The two components rest on different foundations, which is the strongest evidence that they are genuinely distinct. Distance is the affair of Swales's *discourse community*: it governs how much a document must explain. Power is the affair of Weber's *legal-rational authority*: it governs how defensible, self-contained, and evidential a document must be. Distance sets the **scaffolding**; power sets the **armor**.

One power must not be confused with this one. The *recipient's* power over the writer sets the armor. The *writer's* own power — exercised by choosing what to inscribe and what to leave out — is the constitutive floor of §4.2, and it is not a coordinate of a single document's position but a property of the **corpus**: which activities are recorded at all, and whose account becomes the official one. It is taken up in the companion study, where the choice of a classificatory axis (warrant) and its silences are treated together.

### 5.2 The recipient relation rewrites the move structure of a force

The two structures are not merely additive: the recipient relation can *rewrite* how a given force is executed. The clearest case is *prove*. A proof addressed to expert peers may assume a shared method and exhibit only results; the same claim addressed to a non-peer who holds power — an external auditor — must carry its own method on its back, making visible every step that peers would take for granted. The force is constant; the move structure changes with distance and power. That proof is suasive (§4.3) is exactly why: what it takes to win assent depends on *whom* one must convince. This interaction is where the theory becomes operationally demanding, and the companion study treats it directly.

### 5.3 The recipient relation is open, and refinable

Unlike the direction-of-fit families, the recipient relation admits no closure: the social world produces new configurations of distance and power without limit. It can, however, be refined by importing theories of organizational structure. The most pertinent is Elliott Jaques's *Requisite Organization*, which proposes that each level of a hierarchy is defined by a distinctive **time-span of discretion** — the horizon over which a role's decisions mature, from days at the front line to many years at the apex. On this view a document addressed to an operational level is a different act from one addressed to a strategic body, even when both "inform" the same fact, because the recipients' horizons differ.

Jaques is offered strictly as an illustration of how the recipient relation *could* be refined, and with its contestation attached. The theory issued from a long field study (the Glacier Metal project) yet is disputed even by its own collaborators, who doubted whether time-span of discretion is measurable at all. It also exhibits a tension visible to any observer: the horizon a senior role *requires* (years, even decades) routinely diverges from the horizon its incentive structure *rewards* (the quarterly report, the multi-year renewable tenure). That divergence is itself informative — it suggests the documentary horizon of a level is negotiated between what the role requires and what the incentives reward — but it warns against treating any level scheme as more than a heuristic overlay on the recipient relation.

### 5.4 The recipient is, in part, a fiction

One caution keeps the recipient relation from being read as a fact the writer simply reads off the world. The writer has no direct access to the reader; the writer *addresses* an imagined one. Ong's dictum — the writer's audience is always a fiction — and Bakhtin's *addressivity*, that every utterance is shaped by the response it anticipates, both hold that the recipient coordinate is partly a **construction of the act itself**. This does not dissolve the coordinate: a construction can be apt or inept, and misjudging the reader's distance or power is among the commonest documentary failures. But it means the recipient is a position the writer *posits*, and a document can always be read by an actual reader the writer never posited — which is the bridge to §6.

## 6. Inscribed versus activated: the document and its readings

The coordinates of §§4–5 are fixed at the moment of writing. They describe the **position of inscription**. But a document, once it exists, can be read from a position other than the one it was written from — and this is not an edge case but the normal condition of a document that outlives its first use.

Documentation science and archival theory both anticipate this. Briet's evidential document is evidence *within a practice*; change the practice and the evidence changes. Schellenberg's distinction between a record's **primary value** (its use to its creator) and its **secondary value** (its later use to others) is precisely the observation that a document's worth migrates as its readers change. An internal note written to coordinate colleagues has a primary value of coordination; requisitioned later, it acquires a secondary, evidential value it was never written to bear.

Three regimes of re-inscription can be distinguished, and the distinction matters because they carry very different costs.

- **Assumed mono-positional.** The document is written for one position and belongs there. Anticipating other readings would only degrade it. A frank internal note owes its usefulness — and, should it ever be examined, its evidential value — precisely to having been written without an eye to other readers.

- **Declared multi-positional.** The document announces its several uses and is structured for them at writing. A report that is at once a proof for one reader and a reference for another is legitimately double, and this can be designed from the start. *This* is the case the boundary object names properly: an artifact built to be plastic across communities while robust in identity.

- **Suffered re-positionable.** The document is drawn into a position it was not written for — the internal note pulled into an audit or a legal proceeding. This regime has a peculiar property: the possibility of the new reading *cannot be inscribed without destroying the document*. To mark a note as "written in anticipation of audit" is to make it a prepared document, read as such, and so to forfeit exactly the innocence that gave the unprepared note its evidential force. The observation alters the observed.

A word on the mechanism, because a loose appeal to the boundary object would mislead here. The suffered case is often the *opposite* of a boundary object: the document was *not* robust to the new use, and is changed by being drawn into it. Its mechanism is better named by Ferraris and by Freadman. Re-inscription is literal: a **new recording** recontextualizes the old — the note *becomes* evidence at the moment it is entered into the dossier — and this is Freadman's *uptake*, the taking-up of a text by a genre other than the one it was written for. This is also why anticipation destroys the document: to anticipate the audit is already to inscribe something else, and the reader then takes up that other thing.

It follows that the common counsel to *write every message as though it will be read in court* is not prudence but a pathology. It optimizes every document for a position it will almost never occupy, and in doing so removes it from the position it does occupy: the defensive over-armoring of routine communication is a form of illegibility. The stress that this counsel tries to discharge is, in the third regime, **structurally irreducible** — it cannot be dissolved by making the anticipation explicit, because the explicit anticipation breaks the document. A theory that merely sorted documents would miss this; a theory of the inscribed act explains why a certain documentary anxiety has no clean remedy.

Re-inscription is not confined to the recipient relation. A document written to *account* may be read as *proof*; an expressive note may become evidence. Reading can shift a document along the force axis as well. The general principle:

> The coordinates describe the **inscribed** act. Every reading may **re-inscribe** the document into another position. The document does not change; the position of reading does.

## 7. The temporal orientation: a dimension that dissolves

One apparent dimension — the difference between a document that speaks of a **state** of the world and one that speaks of a **change** of state — looked, in earlier drafts, like a third coordinate that resisted placement. It is better resolved by dissolving it, for it is not one coordinate but three things that already have homes.

Part of it is *already in the force axis*. Direction of fit carries a temporal orientation: word-to-world acts face a present or past state, world-to-word acts a future to be brought about, declarations the instant of issue. To that extent "state versus change" adds nothing new.

Part of it is *not a coordinate at all*, but a property of propositional content. That *describe* and *explain* can each face a settled state or a transition is a fact about the *world the act speaks of*, not about the *position from which it is written*. A content-distinction is not an axis.

And the remainder — whether a document accretes, is rewritten in place, or is frozen and superseded; the state-document kept current versus the change-record that freezes — is a property of **trajectory, and of the relation between two entities of the system**, not of the single act. It belongs to the companion study, which places it as the *state–change seam* where the communication and archival systems join. The dimension that seemed a confused third coordinate here resolves, once the unit of analysis is the system, into a clean relation there. Nothing is left over to defer.

## 8. A note on method: closure versus saturation

The recurring temptation in this field is to claim that a set of categories is complete. It should be resisted, and the reason is worth stating as a principle.

A typology can be complete in two very different senses. It is **deductively closed** when it follows from a formal principle external to the analyst's own choices — as the four directions of fit follow from the logically possible relations between words and world. Such closure is rare, and even where it exists it is not beyond dispute. A typology is **empirically saturated** when, after a wide survey, new material stops adding categories — the standard by which grounded theory declares a scheme adequate. Saturation warrants confidence, never finality.

The categories of this theory divide accordingly. The four direction-of-fit families are deductively closed. *Everything finer* — the recognized cells, the perlocutionary intentions above the floor, the configurations of distance and power — is empirically saturated and therefore open. The ambition is that the coordinate *space* can locate any act an organization writes; it is never that the *inventory* of named cells is complete. None of the major frameworks in this domain claims more than saturation, and the one serious bid for closure in the adjacent literature (Searle's five illocutionary classes) has been argued over for forty years. The appropriate posture is to state, for each dimension, which kind of adequacy it has. Asserting closure where it is legitimate strengthens, rather than weakens, the candid refusal to assert it elsewhere.

---

## 9. Existing systems as partial projections

If the model is sound, the documentation systems of real organizations should appear as partial projections of its coordinate space — each lighting some dimensions and neglecting others.

**DITA** refines the *describe* and *instruct* cells for reuse at industrial scale and is essentially silent on deciding, committing, and proving: it is the mature instantiation of one corner of the space. **Diátaxis** projects the whole space onto the formative-and-floor assertive/directive plane — and, as §4.2 showed, its quadrants are exactly the crossing of the formative aim with direction of fit; within that plane it is the best instrument available, and its limit is that it has only that plane, blind to the suasive aim and to the floor. The **Atlassian stack** touches every region but incoherently — issues are proposals and accounts, pull requests are declarative decisions, the wiki is a repository that, lacking a theory of how its genres relate, tends to accumulate into undifferentiated sediment. **GitHub** is the most theory-aligned of the common platforms because it binds force and recipient into its primitives: an issue proposes or accounts, a merged pull request *decides* and changes reality by record, a contributing guide mandates, a signed commit is evidence with an unbroken provenance. **GitLab's** handbook-first culture is the field's most radical experiment — writing made to precede and constitute the work — collapsing describe, mandate, commit, and decide onto one surface under a single-source-of-truth discipline; it demonstrates both the reach of near-total externalization and its cost in governance.

---

## 10. The human forces the theory must respect

A theory of documents that ignored why people decline to write them would be useless. Four frictions are predictable from the foundations.

**Documentation transfers power.** If the file is the seat of authority, externalizing knowledge makes its holder replaceable; silence is rational when expertise is leverage. This is the standing limit of every exhortation to "just write it down," and it has a sharper form in the knowledge-management literature: the celebrated conversion of tacit to explicit knowledge assumes externalization is mostly a matter of method, whereas the original sense of the tacit — *we know more than we can tell* — implies that some expert knowledge cannot be fully externalized at all. A mature practice routes the genuinely tacit to apprenticeship and documents the rest.

**Formality tracks the accountability gradient.** Because documents assign responsibility, their formality rises with the stakes of being wrong — which is to say, with the *power* coordinate of the recipient. The most demanding position in the entire space is the one combining the *prove* force with a reader who holds power, and the scramble that precedes external examination is the accountability gradient made visible.

**Authorship confers identity.** People are attached to documents they have authored because authorship is a claim to having defined the official account. A migration that treats an authored document as mere content to be reformatted meets resistance that has nothing to do with format and everything to do with whose voice the organization recognizes as authoritative.

**Genres are a cognitive economy.** A recognized form lets readers know what to expect and writers skip explanation; discarding familiar genres taxes a whole discourse community. The conservative lesson is to extend and connect existing genres rather than replace them.

---

## 11. Limits and the road ahead

The theory is offered as an instrument, and its limits are part of it. It treats the single document as its unit and therefore underdescribes the **genre system** — the interrelated sequences in which documents engender one another — which may be where much of the real structure lives; that system is grounded here (Miller, Bakhtin, Bazerman) but developed by the companions, not this essay. It says little about the **semiotics of non-textual documents**: a diagram or a dataset has an internal grammar — Peircean, iconic and indexical as much as symbolic — that this essay only acknowledges. It is a modern, bureaucratic, broadly Western theory of the record and does not claim to travel across all cultures of writing. And its one closure rests on a principle that is itself contested.

Two companion studies extend it. The first, *A Rhetoric of Organizational Genres*, takes up the **generative** property of the coordinates (§2): for each cell it develops the move structure that guides writing, the verb that best names it for a writer, the failure mode it courts, and the way distance and power rewrite its execution. It is the bridge from this theory to operational templates.

The second, *The Documentary System*, concerns the **life cycle** of the document — its trajectory from creation through validation, use, obsolescence, archival, and possible requisition. This belongs not to the document but to the *system* that carries it. The position of inscription set out here predicts the trajectory: a record that decides is frozen and archived; one that commits is re-issued in versions; one that describes is maintained; one that proves is re-run against preserved inputs. The static coordinates engender the dynamic life. The state-and-change relation that §7 dissolves out of this essay is seated there, at the seam where the system's two halves join.

---

## 12. Coda

The dominant frameworks succeeded by giving a scattered practice a shared language for one important question — what does a user need? The wager of this essay is that the larger practice, everything an organization writes to externalize its knowledge and bind its commitments, admits a comparable treatment, and that the language already exists, distributed across the philosophy of language, the social ontology of the document, documentation science, the sociology of boundary objects, organizational and linguistic genre theory, distributed cognition, archival science, and the anthropology of the file. The organizational document is not a container to be sorted, and it is never neutral: it is an inscribed act, performed toward a recipient across distance and power, making its content the world and silencing the rest, carrying evidence and authority, and liable at any moment to be taken up from a position other than the one it was written from. Seen this way, the decision record, the audit dossier, the committee minute, and the humble tutorial finally share a single space — and the points the theory cannot yet name still have coordinates within it.
